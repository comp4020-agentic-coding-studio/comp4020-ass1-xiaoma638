A distance case spanning two thousand years

Is the sun closer in the morning, or at noon?

Two children saw two different suns: one bigger, one hotter. But does what you see, or what you feel, really tell you the distance?

Both spoke from what they'd witnessed with their own eyes. So why does the same sun give two different answers?

Bring the case to Confucius ↓

Who's right?

Confucius

The story doesn't hand us an answer

"Confucius could not decide."

The children laughed: "Who said you knew so much?"

That's not a failed answer. Confucius refused to force a hard question into a tidy, intuitive conclusion. From here, we won't take either child's side — instead, we'll test each of the things they actually observed, one at a time.

Start with "looks bigger" ↓

Possibility one · start with size

Is the morning sun really bigger?

Before we jump to distance, let's test the claim itself. Child A said the sun looks "as big as a chariot's canopy" at sunrise — so first: can we trust what our eyes tell us about size?

Visual experiment · the Ebbinghaus illusion

What surrounds something changes how big it looks

The blue circles in the middle are actually identical in size — but the circles around them change how big they look. This is the Ebbinghaus illusion.

Taking the experiment back to the sky

Sunrise vs. noon: one sun, two impressions of size

At sunrise there are rooftops, treetops and the horizon for reference; at noon there's only open sky. Watch both clips, then check your own instinct again.

Clue one: apparent size is worth explaining, but it isn't proof of distance.

Possibility two · start with heat

Is noon hotter because we're closer to the sun?

Child B's sensation was real too. But temperature first tells us where the sun sits in the sky — not how far away it is.

The effect of solar altitude

The higher the sun, the more concentrated the energy

At noon, sunlight passes through less atmosphere, and the same beam covers a smaller patch of ground, so the surface heats up more. That's enough to explain why noon "feels like scalding water" — but it says nothing about how far away the sun actually is.

Clue two: how hot it feels is a signal of the sun's altitude, not a verdict on distance.

Layer one · look at scale first

150 million kilometres: where intuition breaks down

Earth's radius is only 6,371 km, while the average Earth–Sun distance is about 149.6 million km. Moving from morning to noon really does change your position — but only by a sliver of that total distance.

Layer two · freeze the orbit

As Earth rotates toward the sun, noon gains ground

Assume, for a moment, that Earth stays fixed at one point in its orbit. As an observer turns from the oblique angle of morning to face the sun more directly at local solar noon, they gain a small edge in distance — that's rotation's contribution.

Layer three · let Earth move

But Earth never stays still

Earth rotates and, at the same time, follows a roughly elliptical orbit. Over the course of a day, the Earth–Sun distance shifts too — sometimes favouring noon, sometimes pulling the advantage back to morning.
